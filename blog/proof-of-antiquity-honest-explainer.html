What Proof of Antiquity Actually Is — an honest explainer

RustChain is an experimental blockchain that calls its consensus mechanism Proof of Antiquity. The pitch is unusual: old computers are worth more than new ones. This post explains what the system actually does, how the hardware fingerprint works, and where the idea is genuinely interesting — as well as its real limitations. No price talk, no hype.

The basic idea: 1 CPU = 1 vote, weighted by age

Most blockchains reward either computation (Proof of Work), stake (Proof of Stake), or storage (Proof of Storage). RustChain rewards physical existence over time. Every real CPU gets one vote, and that vote is multiplied by how old the hardware is. The maintained example is a 2001 PowerPC G4 earning roughly 2.5x a modern server.

Why would you weight for age? The argument is that a machine which has kept running for 20+ years is a scarce, non-fungible physical asset. You cannot spin up ten thousand copies of a 2001 laptop the way you can spin up ten thousand VPS instances. Age is a kind of proof of reality.

The hardware fingerprint: how it catches VMs

This is the technically most interesting part. The node runs a six-check hardware fingerprint before accepting a miner, looking at physical signals like:

  • CPU identity and instruction behavior
  • Cache timing patterns
  • Clock drift characteristics
  • Thermal behavior

Emulators and VMs have measurable fingerprints: timing distributions differ, thermal sensors behave differently (or trivially), and hardware IDs are spoofable but the combination of signals is hard to fake convincingly. The result: VMs are deliberately penalized, and cloud rentals are largely useless for mining this chain. That is a structural anti-sybil property most other DePIN/consensus designs don't have at the base layer.

Emission model

RustChain has a capped supply of 8.39M RTC. Mining receives roughly 94% of the distribution, with founder, dev, bounty, and community pools making up the rest. Epoch rewards are around 1.5 RTC per 10 minutes, and the chain periodically anchors to a private Ergo chain for checkpointing. The network launched in production in December 2025. RTC is not traded on any exchange, there is no bridge off the chain, and the $0.15 reference rate is a planning rate for bounty sizing — not a market price. That matters: treat any "value" claims with skepticism.

Where it's genuinely interesting

  • Anti-emulation at the consensus layer: cheap Sybil resistance via hardware reality is a real design idea, not a gimmick.
  • E-waste as an asset class: rewarding vintage hardware actually extends the useful life of machines that would otherwise be recycled. That's a defensible environmental angle.
  • No ASIC arms race: you can't build a dedicated chip that pretends to be an old PowerBook; the fingerprint is the consensus input.

Where to be skeptical

  • The network is small, experimental, and largely self-referential. Its utility so far is mostly earning its own token by running its own software.
  • RTC has no external market. Earning it is a bet on the project itself.
  • The fingerprint suite is new and hasn't been battle-tested against determined adversaries at scale (there are open red-team bounties for this exact reason).
  • Anchoring to a private Ergo chain is a centralization caveat worth noting honestly.

Getting started

If you're curious, the entry point is simple:

pip install clawrtc
clawrtc miner start

Docs live at github.com/Scottcjn/Rustchain and rustchain.org. The bounty board where this post is being submitted is at github.com/Scottcjn/rustchain-bounties.

Bottom line

Proof of Antiquity is one of the more original consensus designs in years — not because it's going to change the world, but because it reframes the question from "how much can you compute or store" to "what physical thing actually exists." Whether the network ever becomes useful beyond its own token economy is an open question. But the fingerprinting idea is worth watching regardless of RustChain's fate.
